What happens in the minutes before the market opens decides more of the session than most traders give it credit for, because reacting to a price move without first checking what else is scheduled to cause it is a specific, avoidable kind of unprepared.

---

## Know what's scheduled before price tells you

Checking which data releases, earnings, or central bank speakers are due for the instruments being traded turns a sudden move into something explainable instead of something that looks like the market simply going strange. A GER40 trader who knows German PMI lands at 8am reads the pre-market move very differently to one who doesn't, and only one of them is in a position to judge whether a setup that appears right after is worth trusting.

Some days call for a different question: not "what's scheduled" but "is today even worth trading normally." A US index trader checking the calendar and seeing an FOMC decision or the monthly Non-Farm Payrolls print due mid-session isn't just noting a data point, they're deciding in advance whether today is a reduced-size day, a no-new-entries-after-a-certain-time day, or a normal one. That decision is far easier to make calmly before the open than it is to make once a position is already sitting through the release.

---

## Build a short watchlist, not a tracked-everything list

A handful of instruments that are actively traded, checked properly, beats a long list of markets glanced at without real attention. Something is always moving somewhere, and a watchlist with fifteen instruments on it pulls a trader toward whichever one looks most exciting that morning, often outside the setups the trading plan is built around. Two to four instruments, the ones the plan covers, checked with the calendar and the key levels both in mind, is a more useful starting point than broad market awareness for its own sake.

---

## Mark the levels that matter

Deciding, in a calm moment before the session gets busy, which support, resistance, and session highs or lows matter avoids the weaker version of the same decision made live, under time pressure, with a position already forming in a trader's head. That usually means the previous day's high and low, the week's opening level, and any round number or prior structural level the instrument has respected before. A level marked in advance gets judged on its own merits; a level spotted mid-session competes with the urge to already be in the trade.

---

## Different sessions need different preparation

The same instrument can behave quite differently depending on which session is open, covered in more detail in [Trading Sessions](/learn/foundations/trading-sessions/). A trader working the London open is preparing for a different kind of volatility, often a sharp initial move as European liquidity arrives, than one working the London/New York overlap, where both major sessions are live at once and moves can extend further than either session manages alone. Preparation that doesn't account for which session is being traded is preparing for a generic day that doesn't quite match the one about to happen.

---

## Confirm the plan's rules for today specifically

A trading plan is written once, but not every day is the same day it was written for. Reconfirming the day's risk limit, and checking whether today's calendar makes any of the plan's usual setups less reliable, say, skipping a breakout strategy on a morning with a major data release likely to cause a fakeout, takes a couple of minutes and avoids assuming yesterday's plan applies unchanged today.

---

## Key takeaways

- Checking the calendar before the session turns a sudden move into something explainable instead of something confusing
- A major scheduled event, like FOMC or Non-Farm Payrolls, is a decision point in itself: reduced size, no new entries, or a normal day, decided before the open
- A short watchlist of the instruments the plan covers beats broad awareness of everything that's moving
- Marking key levels in a calm moment beats spotting them live, under pressure, with a position already forming
- The same instrument trades differently by session, preparation should match which session is open
- The plan's rules still need a quick daily check, today's calendar can make an otherwise valid setup less reliable
