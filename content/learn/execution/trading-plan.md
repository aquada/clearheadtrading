## A document, not a memory

Something "known in your head" and something written down behave very differently under pressure. A vague intention is easy to bend in the moment it's tested, because there's nothing concrete to check it against. A written rule, entry criteria worded specifically enough to say yes or no, is much harder to quietly reinterpret mid-trade. Writing the plan down doesn't make a trader more disciplined by itself, but it removes one of the easiest ways discipline slips: not remembering, in the moment, what was decided in advance.

## What belongs in a plan, section by section

A working plan is specific enough that two different days produce the same decision given the same chart. That takes ten sections, each with a real, specific answer instead of a general intention. The example running through each one below is for a breakout strategy on a European index, GER40, London session, to show what "specific" looks like in practice.

### 1. Goals and motivation

What the account is for, and over what timeframe, matters more than it looks like it should. A plan built around turning a small account into a full-time income within three months sets entry and risk decisions under pressure that a plan built around steady account growth over two years never has to deal with. Writing the real goal down, and a concrete, honest picture of what success looks like in a year, keeps later decisions, particularly around risk per trade, tied to something realistic instead of something desperate.

> **Example:** *Grow a $10,000 account by roughly 3-5% a month over the next 12 months while working a full-time job, treating any month of outright loss as normal variance, not a signal to increase size.*

### 2. Trading style and schedule

The style, day trading, swing trading, or position trading, decides almost everything else that follows, including which sections of this course apply. A day-trading plan needs defined session hours; a swing-trading plan needs rules for holding through overnight gaps that a day trader never has to think about.

> **Example:** *Day trading, London session only, 08:00–11:30 CET. Three sessions a week minimum, based on realistic availability around a day job.*

### 3. Markets and instruments

"Forex" or "indices" isn't specific enough to build entry criteria around. The instruments need naming, along with the reason they were chosen, liquidity, familiarity, or how well they fit the sessions being traded.

> **Example:** *GER40 only. High liquidity during the London session, familiar price behaviour from six months of demo trading, and it doesn't require staying up for the US close.*

### 4. Entry criteria

This is the heart of the plan: the exact, objective conditions that make a setup valid, worded specifically enough that two different traders looking at the same chart would make the same call. It's just as important to say what doesn't count, even when it looks close, see [Entry Execution](/learn/execution/entry-execution/) for why a "close enough" entry is really a different, worse trade.

> **Example:** *A clean 15-minute close above a pre-marked resistance level, on volume visibly higher than the prior four candles. A wick above the level that closes back below it does not count, regardless of how strong it looks intraday.*

### 5. Exit rules: stop and target

Where the stop goes and where the target comes from need the same specificity as the entry. [Stop Loss Placement](/learn/risk-management/stop-loss-placement/) covers why this should be structural instead of a fixed number of points, and [Risk to Reward Ratio](/learn/risk-management/risk-to-reward-ratio/) covers how the target gets decided.

> **Example:** *Stop below the swing low that preceded the breakout. Target 2R, or the next marked structural level, whichever comes first.*

### 6. Position sizing and risk per trade

The risk percentage and the calculation that turns it into an actual position size, covered in full in [Position Sizing](/learn/risk-management/position-sizing/), belongs here as a fixed number, not a range that flexes with how good a setup looks.

> **Example:** *0.5% of account equity per trade, position size calculated from the stop distance in points using the account's per-point value.*

### 7. Daily and weekly loss limits

A single number decided in advance, along with a clear rule for what happens once it's hit, see [Daily Loss Limits](/learn/risk-management/daily-loss-limits/) for why this has to be set before a losing streak starts, not during one.

> **Example:** *1.5% daily loss limit, stop trading for the day if hit. Weekly limit of 4%, stop trading for the week if hit. Maximum two trades per day regardless of outcome.*

### 8. Trade management rules

What happens once a trade is open and moving, covered in [Managing the Trade](/learn/execution/managing-the-trade/): when the stop moves to breakeven, whether any partial exits are taken, and at what point, decided now instead of negotiated live.

> **Example:** *Stop moves to breakeven once price reaches 1R in favour. No partial exits, full position runs to the 2R target or the structural level, whichever comes first.*

### 9. Record-keeping and review

What gets logged after every trade and how often the log gets reviewed, covered in [Trade Journaling](/learn/execution/trade-journaling/) and [Post-Session Review](/learn/execution/post-session-review/).

> **Example:** *Every trade logged same-day: setup, planned versus actual entry/stop/target, size, and the reason for entry in the moment. Reviewed weekly against the plan's own criteria.*

### 10. When this plan does not apply

The situations where no trade should be taken regardless of setup quality: major scheduled news, illiquid hours, a broken platform connection, or anything else the plan needs to name explicitly. A plan silent on its own limits gets stretched to cover situations it was never built for.

> **Example:** *No new entries within 30 minutes of a German or eurozone data release rated high-impact. No trading in the first five minutes after the London open, spreads are unreliable.*

The **downloadable template above** covers all ten sections in a blank, fillable format, the fastest way to start is filling that in directly instead of building the structure from scratch. Babypips' trading-plan lesson is also a well-known, free resource for the shape of one, and several brokers and prop firms publish their own free templates in their education sections. None of them will fit without changes, a template built for a swing trader doesn't suit a scalper's entry criteria, but starting from a structure that already asks the right questions is faster than inventing the format from nothing.

---

## It needs testing before it needs following

A plan on paper is a hypothesis, not a proven edge, until it's been checked against real trades or historical data. Committing hard to an untested plan simply because it's written down and looks professional mistakes structure for validity, two different things. A plan earns the confidence to follow it strictly through a track record, not through how organised it looks on the page.

---

## Key takeaways

- A working plan needs ten specific sections: goals, style and schedule, markets, entry criteria, exit rules, position sizing, loss limits, trade management, record-keeping, and when it doesn't apply
- Each section needs a real, specific answer, "grow the account steadily" and "0.5% risk per trade" do real work; "trade well" and "manage risk sensibly" don't
- The downloadable template covers all ten sections in a fillable format, a faster starting point than building the structure from nothing
- Writing it down removes one of the easiest ways discipline slips: forgetting, under pressure, what was already decided
- A plan is a hypothesis until tested against real results, not something to follow strictly just because it's written down
