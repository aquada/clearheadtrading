Recognising the moment a session has stopped being productive, before it turns into a session that actively costs money, is its own skill, separate from having good rules to trade by in the first place.

---

## The rule-based reasons to stop

Some stopping points are mechanical and decided in advance: the daily loss limit is hit, a set number of trades for the day has been taken, or a fixed session end time has arrived. None of these need judging in the moment, they're triggers written into the plan precisely so the decision to stop doesn't depend on how the trader feels about stopping right then.

---

## The signs it's not about the rules yet

Some warning signs show up before any rule-based limit is technically reached: focus visibly slipping, entries starting to feel rushed, a string of setups that don't quite meet the bar getting taken anyway. None of these trip a formal limit on their own, but waiting for the rule to trigger before acting on what's already visible gives up ground that didn't need to be given up.

---

## Stopping isn't a failure, continuing past this point is the actual risk

A trader who stops early on a day that wasn't working protected the account as intended, that's the plan doing its job, not the plan failing. The account gets hurt by the session that continues past this point, looking for a good trade that was never going to arrive that day. Reframing an early stop as a successful outcome, not a disappointing one, makes it much easier to act on.

---

## Key takeaways

- Some stopping points are mechanical and decided in advance: a daily loss limit, a trade count, or a fixed session end time
- Other warning signs, slipping focus, rushed entries, weaker setups getting taken anyway, show up before any formal limit is hit
- Stopping early on a bad day is the plan working correctly, not a failure of it
- The real risk is the session that continues past this point, not the one that stops on time
