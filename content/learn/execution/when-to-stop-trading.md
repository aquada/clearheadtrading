Recognising the moment a session has stopped being productive, before it turns into a session that actively costs money, is its own skill, separate from having good rules to trade by in the first place.

---

## The rule-based reasons to stop

Some stopping points are mechanical and decided in advance: the daily loss limit is hit, a set number of trades for the day has been taken, or a fixed session end time has arrived. None of these need judging in the moment, they're triggers written into the plan precisely so the decision to stop doesn't depend on how the trader feels about stopping right then.

*Example: a trader with a 1.5% daily loss limit is down 1.5% by 10am after two losing trades that both followed the plan correctly. The limit says stop. There's no third trade to consider, no case for "just one more to get it back", the rule already made the decision before the day started.*

---

## The signs it's not about the rules yet

Some warning signs show up before any rule-based limit is technically reached: focus visibly slipping, entries starting to feel rushed, a string of setups that don't quite meet the bar getting taken anyway. None of these trip a formal limit on their own, but waiting for the rule to trigger before acting on what's already visible gives up ground that didn't need to be given up.

---

## Three different kinds of stopping

Not every stop is the same size. Stopping for the day, the most common case, is a single session's decision and resets the next morning with nothing carried over. Stopping a specific setup or strategy is a narrower call, made when a particular kind of trade keeps underperforming what a proper review shows it should be doing, and it lasts until that review is done, not just until the mood passes. Stopping altogether for a longer break, days or weeks instead of the rest of a session, is the response to something bigger than one bad stretch, the kind of sustained decision-quality drop covered in [Burnout](/learn/psychology/burnout/). Treating all three as the same "stop trading" instinct misses that they call for very different lengths of time away.

---

## The "just one more trade" trap

The pull to take one more trade after a limit is hit, or after a session has clearly stopped working, rarely announces itself as recklessness. It shows up as a reasonable-sounding case for an exception: this setup is different, this one's too good to skip, today's just been unlucky, not a real off day. That reasoning is the same pattern covered in [Revenge Trading](/learn/psychology/revenge-trading/) and [Overtrading](/learn/psychology/overtrading/), applied specifically to the decision of whether to stop instead of to any single trade. A rule that says stop doesn't get a vote from the trade that's tempting to take anyway.

---

## Stopping isn't a failure, continuing past this point is the actual risk

A trader who stops early on a day that wasn't working protected the account as intended, that's the plan doing its job, not the plan failing. The account gets hurt by the session that continues past this point, looking for a good trade that was never going to arrive that day. Reframing an early stop as a successful outcome, not a disappointing one, makes it much easier to act on.

---

## Key takeaways

- Some stopping points are mechanical and decided in advance: a daily loss limit, a trade count, or a fixed session end time
- Other warning signs, slipping focus, rushed entries, weaker setups getting taken anyway, show up before any formal limit is hit
- A session stop, a strategy stop, and a longer break are three different sizes of stopping, not the same instinct at different volumes
- The urge to take "just one more trade" after a limit is hit is the same pattern as revenge trading and overtrading, aimed at the decision to stop itself
- Stopping early on a bad day is the plan working correctly, not a failure of it
- The real risk is the session that continues past this point, not the one that stops on time
