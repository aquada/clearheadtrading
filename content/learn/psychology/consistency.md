One good trade proves almost nothing about whether a strategy works, and one bad trade proves almost as little, which makes consistency, doing the same process the same way regardless of the last result, worth more over time than any single outcome ever will.

---

## Why one result tells you so little

Every strategy with a real edge still loses a meaningful share of the time, and every strategy without one can still win several trades in a row purely by chance. Randomness alone guarantees stretches that look like skill and stretches that look like failure, on both a good process and a bad one, over any short enough window. Judging a strategy, or a trader, by the last handful of trades mistakes noise for signal almost every time.

---

## What consistency looks like day to day

In practice it's less about willpower and more about repetition: the same criteria for an entry, the same risk per trade, the same process for deciding when to walk away, applied after three wins in a row the same way it's applied after three losses. It's not exciting, and it doesn't produce a good story after a single session, but it's what lets a real edge, if the strategy has one, show up in the results over enough trades for the maths to work in its favour.

---

## The trap of changing the process based on the last result

The hardest version of this shows up after a loss, when the temptation is to add a filter, tighten a rule, or size down specifically because of what just happened, treating one outcome as proof the process needs fixing. A process built on solid logic doesn't need adjusting because of normal variance; it needs adjusting when a genuine flaw in the logic itself is found, which is a different, slower kind of review than a single bad trade can provide.

---

## Key takeaways

- One trade, win or lose, is far too small a sample to judge a strategy by
- Consistency means applying the same entry, risk, and exit rules regardless of how recent trades went
- Randomness alone produces winning and losing streaks on both good and bad strategies
- Change a process because of a genuine flaw found through proper review, not because of the last result
